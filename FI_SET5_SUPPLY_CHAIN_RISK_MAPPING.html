Part 1: AI-Mapped Risk Zones — Pilot Partner Corridor

Zone 1 — Rustenburg N4 Origin Corridor 01 Rustenburg → Skilpadshek / Botswana Border

Platinum Highway — Depot Siphoning and Overnight Risk

The route originates in Rustenburg, the heart of South Africa's platinum mining belt. Heavy commercial vehicles, contractor fleets, and mining support trucks create a crowded operating environment with frequent unsecured stops. Documented risk: overnight siphoning at depots, industrial yards, and along the R104 alternative route (bypasses N4 toll gates). Single truck losing 50L/week in this zone = R65,000/year. North West Province has flagged fuel theft as a growing operational risk for mining-sector fleet operators.

Zone 2 — Botswana Transit Corridor 02 Skilpadshek → Plumtree Border (Botswana Transit to Zimbabwe)

900km+ Transit Leg — GSM Gaps, Fuel Card Fraud, Detour Risk

Many Rustenburg-to-Harare routes transit Botswana to bypass Beitbridge congestion. This introduces: limited cellular coverage in sections (edge buffering required), driver collusion risk (siphon at depots along corridor, submit fraudulent fuel receipts), and reduced telematics visibility when crossing SA-Botswana network boundaries. Fuel theft here is lower frequency but higher volume per event. Organized criminal activity along this transit corridor has been documented.

Zone 3 — Zimbabwe Border Entry and Harare Destination 03 Plumtree / Beitbridge → Harare, Zimbabwe

Border Crossing + Destination Risk — Highest Severity

Trucks wait 24–72 hours at Zimbabwe border crossings. During this time, vehicles are parked in open areas with limited security — vulnerable to organized siphoning. Operators report single-event losses of up to 600 litres at these crossings. Zimbabwe is a preferred destination for smuggled fuel due to high local prices and currency constraints — creating organized demand for stolen diesel. Inside Zimbabwe, vehicles face additional risk at Harare destination depots and during customs clearance. SA-to-Zimbabwe fuel smuggling is well-documented by Zimbabwe Revenue Authority.

Part 2: Logistics Manager Pain Points

"We get the alert. Fuel dropped while the truck was parked at the border. That's all we know. We can't report it, we can't act on it, and we definitely can't use it in a disciplinary hearing. The driver says nothing happened. We're left guessing."

The core pain is not detection — it's proof. Fleet managers with sensors and telematics still cannot distinguish real theft from sensor malfunction, temperature-related tank contraction, or dual-tank fuel shift on a grade.

On dual-tank N4 operations: "Our trucks run dual tanks through Rustenburg and up the N4. When they park on any kind of incline — and there are plenty on that route — the fuel shifts between tanks and sets off every theft alert we have. We get 30–50 false alerts a week just from that. After a while, the team stops looking at the alerts entirely. That's when real theft slips through."

On month-end reconciliation: "Finance wants a number. The number keeps being 5–8% higher than it should be. We report 'theft and variance' and that's where the conversation ends. Nobody can explain it. We need a document we can hand to someone and say 'this is what happened.'"

Part 3: Risk Zone Prioritization for PhyLo's Detection Engine

Priority 1 — Highest Immediate Value

1 Dual-Tank Grade Attribution (Engine Foundation Model) — Manager pain is immediate: 30–50 false alerts per week from dual-tank fuel shift on parked inclines. Build the dual-tank, parked-on-grade physics model first. If PhyLo eliminates these false alerts while maintaining theft detection, the ops team adopts it within the first week of the pilot. This is the clearest demonstration of what physics-informed means vs rule-based systems.

Priority 2 — Highest Severity Per Event

2 Cross-Border Stationary Attribution (Zone 3) — Extended stationary periods at the Zimbabwe border (24–72 hours). Single events cause 600L+ losses = R17,000+ per incident. Engine must: flag fuel drops during extended stationary periods with high confidence, attribute drops outside geofenced safe zones, and account for thermal tank contraction overnight. Botswana transit leg: use CAN bus and odometer data to substitute for GPS when cellular coverage drops.

Priority 3 — Full Corridor Model

3 Route-Level Consumption Validation (Full Rustenburg → Harare Corridor) — 800km+ route requires physics-based fuel burn modeling: expected consumption = f(grade profile, distance, load, temperature). Replace threshold alerts with a calibrated model. Deviations are classified, not just flagged. The Month-End Fuel Audit — the winning product from Ideaster testing — is the output of this model. Finance directors are the primary buyer.
